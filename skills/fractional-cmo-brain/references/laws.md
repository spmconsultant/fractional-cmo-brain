# Laws

Laws describe how marketing works whether you like it or not.

**The test for a law:** a smart competitor is forced to agree it's simply true. If a competitor could reasonably disagree, it's a principle or a strategy, not a law.

**How a law is earned:** a result shows up in a live campaign, repeats across different clients and industries, and passes the test above. Then every audit, budget and campaign decision is checked against it.

When answering, name the law by number and title, say what the user is doing that breaks it, and give the fix.

---

## Law 1. Two Meanings of ROI
**Measure only the ratio and you miss what actually changed because you spent the money.**

- *Return on Investment* is a backward-looking financial ratio: (Return − Investment) / Investment. It's useful for budgeting and reporting, but weak for decisions.
- *Result of Investment* is what changed because you invested: more qualified demand, a shorter sales cycle, a higher conversion rate, more price tolerance, stronger recall or trust.
- Two campaigns can show the same financial ROI and very different results. Retargeting at 500% may only capture demand that already existed, while brand education at 200% may raise close rates and shorten the cycle.
- **How to apply:** measure incrementality, not attribution (holdout tests, geo splits, budget on/off tests). Track system-level levers such as conversion rate, velocity, retention, price realization and CAC/LTV. Tie results to financial levers, e.g. "a 3% close-rate increase at constant lead volume."

## Law 2. The Four Core Marketing Abilities
**Miss one of the four and the sale never happens, no matter how strong the other three are.**

| Ability | What the buyer believes | Barrier it removes | Without it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depend | You'll reliably deliver with acceptable risk | Risk | Buyers delay or default to the incumbent |
| Like | Positive feeling toward the brand | Emotional resistance | Buyers choose on price alone |
| Relate | "This is for someone like me" | Relevance | Buyers disengage, even from a strong product |
| Knowledge | They understand what you do and why it matters | Confusion | Buyers can't justify the decision |

A purchase happens when all four are present. Over-investing in one can't make up for a missing one.

## Law 3. Leverage Marketing Strategy
**Scale spend before you find leverage and you only make the problem bigger.**

- Leverage means taking a proven performance driver and making small, controlled changes to increase its output without adding much cost or risk.
- A 5–10% improvement at the right point (a high-traffic page, a high-close-rate audience, a high-performing message) can beat a 50–100% increase in spend.
- Leverage types, from highest to lowest impact: message (headline framing, proof order, risk reversal), experience (page flow, form friction, CTA clarity), audience (narrower targeting, excluding low-intent groups), offer (guarantees, trials, pricing structure), and spend last. More spend amplifies any problem upstream.
- **How to apply:** find the constraint. Change **one variable at a time**, measure the lift against baseline, and lock the gain before moving on. Change two things at once and you'll never know which one worked or broke it.
- Example: same 10,000 visits, conversion 2% → 2.5% and close rate 20% → 22% = 15 more deals with no extra traffic.

## Law 4. The Law of Platitudes
**What everyone agrees with persuades no one.**

- The safer and more consensus-driven a message gets, the less persuasive it is. Platitudes come from committee editing, trying to appeal to everyone, and fear of excluding anyone.
- Examples: "We put customers first," "Quality service you can trust," "Your partner for success," "Family owned and operated."
- **The test:** could a direct competitor say this without changing a word? If yes, delete it.
- **The fix:** make claims specific, risk-bearing, verifiable, segmented and opinionated. Replace adjectives with mechanisms ("powerful" → how; "scalable" → to what limit). Use proof, not promises.
- The cost: no contrast, higher CPAs, weak recall, price competition, and long sales cycles because sales has to do the persuading.

## Law 5. The Law of Thirds
**Work backward from the deals you need, or your lead targets are fiction.**

- Plan from closed deals backward: Leads = Deals ÷ close rate. Clicks = Leads ÷ click-to-lead rate. Impressions = Clicks ÷ impression-to-click rate.
- The idealized "thirds" model (30% at each stage) is a planning frame, not a benchmark. Realistic ranges: impression→click 0.5–3%, click→lead 10–30%, lead→close 20–35%.
- Example: 30 deals at a 30% close rate = 100 leads; at 20% click→lead = 500 clicks; at 2% impression→click = 25,000 impressions.
- Small improvements early in the funnel cut required volume a lot. Raising impression→click from 2% to 3% cuts the impressions needed by about a third.

## Law 6. The Law of Diminishing Returns
**Blended numbers hide the exact dollar that stopped working.**

- With strategy, creative and audience unchanged, each added dollar produces less than the one before. The causes are audience saturation, frequency inflation, auction pressure and creative fatigue.
- Blended CPA can look fine while marginal CPA collapses. Example: $10k → 200 leads ($50), $20k → 340 ($59 blended), $30k → 420 ($71 blended), but the third $10k cost $125 per lead.
- **Signs you're past the optimum:** CPA rising faster than volume, frequency above 3–5, revenue flat while spend climbs.
- **Rule:** scale until *incremental* CPA reaches your allowable CPA, not until blended numbers look bad. Refresh creative before raising budget, separate high- and low-intent audiences, and use incrementality tests.

## Law 7. The Girlfriend Effect
**Lead with your name and logo, and they leave before they care who you are.**

- Relevance comes before identity. A buyer won't care about your company name, logo, tagline, founding year or credentials until you've given them a reason to.
- **The First-Sentence Test:** cover the opening line of any homepage, ad, email or pitch. If it names the company, the founder, the years in business or the service category before something the reader wants, it fails.
- Example: "ABC Roofing: Quality You Can Trust" fails. "Your roof passed inspection in 2019. Insurers changed the standard in 2024." passes.
- Rewriting the first sentence costs nothing in media. It's the highest-leverage change to make before any budget increase. Put the name right after relevance is established, where it carries weight.

## Law 8. The Check-Writing Test
**If the owner can't name the number when they sign the check, the account is already lost.**

- Every marketing investment needs one number the owner is watching, agreed before the money moves. If the owner can't say what they're paying to get, any result will feel like a loss.
- Law 15 (Don't Trust the Zero) is the data discipline behind this law.

## Law 9. Pricing Obscure
**A round price reads like a guess. A precise one reads like it was worked out.**

- $5,000 sounds picked, and invites "why not $4,000?" $4,870 sounds calculated: the buyer assumes hours, materials and margin behind it. $4,999 isn't precise. It reads as $5,000 with a costume on.
- A lower entry price creates commitment. A buyer who says yes at a smaller number is more likely to keep saying yes.
- The odd number should have real math behind it. An odd price on a bad offer is still a bad offer.

## Law 10. Outcome Without Consequence
**A headline that names no consequence tells the reader nothing.**

- "Stop backups now" says nothing about what happens if you don't. "We can fix your pipes without tearing out your floors" names the damage the reader fears.
- Buyers aren't afraid of slowness. They're afraid of damage to their house, data, payroll or weekend. Pain and consequence beat speed and features.
- The image has to tell the same story as the headline. A headline about floors next to a picture of a pipe kills the ad.
- More examples: "Managed IT for growing businesses" → "When the server goes down at 2 p.m., so does payroll." "Same-day AC service" → "Get it fixed before the July heat turns a repair into a replacement."

## Law 11. The Default Wins
**Showing up consistently beats being clever once in a while.**

- Most business buying relationships are inherited, not chosen. Buyers keep vendors out of habit, because nobody ever put switching in front of them.
- **Selling against an incumbent:** don't argue you're better. Make the first step tiny and low-risk (one order, one job, one sample), then climb from trial to proof to a standing relationship.
- **The test:** ask the buyer "why do you use who you use?" If the answer is "we always have," the door is open.
- One touch won't break a habit. The ladder and consistent follow-up do.

## Law 12. Stale Proof Is False Proof
**An old stat tells buyers nothing is working now.**

- Review counts, years of experience, client totals and service areas drift once published. When they drift, specificity starts eroding trust instead of building it.
- AI search quotes the number on your page as fact, so stale numbers spread.
- **The fix:** anchor proof to facts that don't expire ("since 2008," not "18 years of experience"), and sweep every public number (website, Google profile, social bios) once a quarter.

## Law 13. Fresh Data or No Data
**A strategy you stop updating stops producing anything worth deciding on.**

- A campaign unchanged for 90 days isn't proven, it's frozen. It gives the same answer every month while frequency climbs and cost per lead creeps up.
- Data only becomes fresh when something changes. Change one thing, watch the result, and the account tells you something new.
- "Let's give it more time" followed by "increase the budget" means putting more money behind a strategy nobody has tested in a quarter.

## Law 14. Clarity of Conversions
**A conversion isn't a lead until you know what it's set to measure.**

- "Conversion" means whatever someone configured it to mean. Accounts often mix real leads (forms, calls) with engagement goals (page views, scroll depth, time on site).
- Engagement goals are legitimate. They show Google the site is credible. They're never leads.
- **Before reading any conversion number,** identify what every conversion action measures. Example: 300 "conversions" at $3.33 each can be 10 real leads at $100 each.

## Law 15. Don't Trust the Zero
**Zero conversions doesn't mean zero results. Shut it off and find out the hard way.**

- "We got zero leads" is a claim until verified. Owners remember the leads that closed and forget the rest.
- **Three rules:** count leads from the CRM yourself, never from memory. Read the trend, not the absolute: going from two prospects a month to four is a 100% lift. Explain every spike, and scrub spam and junk referral traffic before reading the report.
- Buyers often visit 3–5 times before they call. Shut the ads off, and leads that "came from somewhere else" can drop too.
- If the zero is real, own it and diagnose in order: are you being seen, is the traffic real, are visitors engaging, and only then the offer and call to action.

## Law 16. Don't Trust the Fraction
**You can't have 0.44 of a customer. Something fake is being counted.**

- A decimal in a conversion count (0.44, 12.62) means the platform is counting something other than people, usually engagement events or attribution that splits credit.
- Fractions get rolled into cost-per-lead figures that win budget arguments. Example: $1,000 / 12.62 "conversions" = $79 per "lead," but 4 real leads = $250 per lead.
- **The check:** look for the decimal. Find what's being tracked and move it out of the lead count.

## Law 17. Own the Keys
**If someone else holds your accounts, they hold your business.**

- The business must hold owner-level access to the domain registrar, website hosting, Google Business Profile, Google Analytics, Search Console, Google Ads, Meta Business Manager and the CRM.
- When an agency or developer owns the accounts, years of performance data leave with them, and the next team pays to relearn it.
- Owner access comes before any audit, strategy or budget decision. Ask without confrontation: "someone is helping me review performance."

## Law 18. Speed to Lead
**The first business to respond usually wins the job.**

- A buyer who reaches out is shopping now, often with two or three companies at once. The first good answer gets the conversation.
- **Companion principle, Data Before Speed:** speed wins only once the data is right. Every lead lands in one place, its source is tracked, and the team has agreed in writing what a qualified lead is. A fast response to the wrong lead, or to a lead nobody can find, is a fast mistake.
- A slow response doesn't save money. The ad spend is already gone, and every lost lead must be replaced at full price.

## Law 19. AI Cites What It Can Quote and Verify
**If AI can't quote your answer and confirm it somewhere else, you're not in the answer.**

- AI assistants don't rank pages. They assemble one answer from passages they can lift cleanly and confirm elsewhere. A page can rank well and still never get cited.
- **The 150-Word Test:** cover everything except the first 150 words of the page and ask whether an AI could answer the searcher's question from that alone.
- Be quotable (state the answer plainly, early) and confirmable (say the same thing about your business everywhere). An AI that finds three versions of your business trusts none of them.
